Week 2Nine questions: mixed layer, T-S structure and heat budget

What I learned

  • The nine questions from the lecture slides centre on the mixed layer and T-S structure. Mixed-layer depth is not a fixed number: wind mixing typically gives 20–100 m, up to 80–120 m in subpolar winters, while cooling-driven deep convection can reach several hundred metres.
  • The subtropical North Pacific is three-layered: mixed layer (20–40 m, 25–29 ℃) → main thermocline (100–800 m) → near-uniform deep water; salinity peaks at 150–200 m with 34.8–35.2.
  • Surface salinity is set by evaporation minus precipitation (E−P): E>P in the subtropics creates a salinity maximum, while P>E near the ITCZ creates a low-salinity band.
  • Potential temperature: temperature after removing the effect of adiabatic compression, which is what makes water at different depths comparable. Potential density works the same way and must state its reference pressure.
  • Mixed-layer heat budget has four terms: surface heat flux (net shortwave − longwave + sensible + latent), advection, entrainment/detrainment, and the storage term.

Week 3EOF principal component analysis

What I learned

  • Every grid point of an SST field changes with time; EOF splits that variability into a spatial pattern times a time coefficient.
  • Mode 1 maps onto the El Niño pattern, and its time series says when the pattern is strong or weak; modes are mutually uncorrelated and ordered by explained variance.
  • I built an 18-second animation in the 3Blue1Brown style: first why the field can be split this way, then how it is computed.
  • Along the way I deployed this site to GitHub and got a public link.

Related: EOF principal component analysis　·　How EOF is computed (18 s)

What I did not get → how I got it

At firstEOF hands me a set of spatial patterns and a set of time coefficients. The patterns I can look at, but the time coefficient is just a curve going up and down — what does it actually say? Is it a temperature? What do positive and negative mean?

LaterThe time coefficient is not another physical quantity; it is how much of that pattern is present at each moment. Subtract the mean field, project the anomaly onto the first spatial pattern, and you get the coefficient for that moment: positive means the SST pattern resembles the warm phase (El Niño-like), negative means the opposite phase (La Niña-like), and the larger the magnitude the closer the match. Its units match the original field (dimensionless after standardisation), so only pattern × coefficient gives the field back.

Week 4From seawater density to geostrophic velocity

What I learned

  • Inertial flow: once the wind stops, a water parcel keeps circling under the Coriolis force, whose strength changes with latitude and sets the direction of deflection.
  • Ekman depth: the scale over which the wind's influence decays with depth.
  • Density to velocity takes four steps: density profile → tilted isopycnals → specific-volume anomaly and dynamic height (integrated) → sloping surface and pressure gradient → geostrophic velocity.
  • Fig. 7.11 is one observed example of exactly this chain: density section → specific-volume anomaly → dynamic height → eastward geostrophic velocity.

Related: From density to geostrophic velocity (with an interactive quiz)

What I did not get → how I got it

At firstWhy does dynamic height have to be integrated? Can't a density anomaly in one layer tell you how high the sea surface is?

LaterSea-surface height is the accumulated contribution of specific-volume anomalies over all layers — large near the surface, small at depth, but every layer counts. Looking at a single layer is looking at one term of that sum: layers differ in thickness, so equal anomalies do not contribute equally. You have to integrate layer by layer before you can say how much the surface is raised.

Week 5Sverdrup balance, Ekman pumping and potential vorticity

What I learned

  • The core is conservation of potential vorticity: a stretched water column moves poleward, a squashed one moves equatorward.
  • The full chain: wind → surface convergence/divergence → stretching/squashing → to conserve vorticity the column is pushed poleward/equatorward → a broad interior Sverdrup flow → closed by a strong western boundary current.
  • Ekman pumping is the step that turns wind-stress curl into vertical motion.

What I did not get → how I got it

At firstWhat is Sverdrup balance actually saying? Why would wind make the whole basin transport water in one direction?

LaterThe key is conservation of potential vorticity: because potential vorticity is conserved, a stretched column moves poleward and a squashed column moves equatorward. Combine that constraint with the surface convergence/divergence forced by the wind and you get the interior Sverdrup transport — it is not that the wind blows water across the basin, but that the column is forced north or south to keep its vorticity.
